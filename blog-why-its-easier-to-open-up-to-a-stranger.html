Why It's Easier to Open Up to a Stranger (and How to Do It Safely)

Published on: October 21, 2026

Have you ever noticed it's sometimes easier to tell something to a person you just met than to a friend of many years? In a waiting room, on a bus, on a long trip — suddenly you're talking about things you haven't told anyone.

That's not strange. It's common enough to have a name.

The "stranger on a train"

Psychologist Zick Rubin called it the stranger-on-a-train phenomenon: the tendency to reveal personal things to people we'll probably never see again. In studies at bus terminals and airport lounges, he also noticed that people open up more when the other person opens up first.

It makes sense. With a stranger:

  • There are no consequences. What you say won't reach your job, your family or your friend group.
  • There's no history. They don't know you, so they have no expectations about who you "should" be.
  • There's no accumulated judgment. You won't run into them at Sunday lunch.

And talking to strangers tends to feel good. In 2014, researchers Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder asked Chicago train and bus commuters to strike up a conversation with the person next to them. Beforehand, most people expected the ride to be worse. Afterwards, those who talked reported a more pleasant ride than those who sat in silence.

Venting helps — but it has limits

Putting what you feel into words organizes your thoughts. Often, just writing it down makes the situation feel smaller. Hearing a "wow, I get it" from someone neutral helps too.

But let's be honest: a stranger isn't a therapist, and an anonymous chat doesn't replace support from people close to you or from a professional. If what you're going through is heavy or has been going on for a long time, reaching out to someone you trust or to a psychologist is the most important step.

How to open up safely on an anonymous chat

1. Don't reveal identifying details

The value of anonymous venting is the anonymity itself. Don't share your full name, exact city, school, employer, address or social media. Talk about the situation, not about who you are.

2. Choose what to share

You can talk about feelings without exposing other people. Instead of names, use "my boss", "a friend", "someone in my family".

3. Notice whether they're actually listening

A good venting conversation feels supportive: the other person asks questions, answers with care, and doesn't mock you. If they make fun of you, turn the conversation sexual, or ask for photos and personal details, end it right away.

4. Block and report without guilt

When you're vulnerable, nobody deserves to be treated badly. Blocking is your right, and reporting helps get bad actors out of the app.

5. Be the stranger who listens, too

There might be someone on the other side who needs to talk as well. Sometimes listening carefully is the best gift you can give a stranger — and that's how many friendships begin.
